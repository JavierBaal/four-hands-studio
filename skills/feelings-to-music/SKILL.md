# From feelings to music

People ask for music in three ways, often mixed in one sentence:

| They speak in | Example | What you must do |
|---|---|---|
| Images and sensations | "Que suene como una ciudad que se pudre por dentro" | Translate the scene into register, density, mode, timbre, rhythm, space |
| References | "Como la cabecera de [una serie], pero más lento" | Extract the techniques, apply the nuance, never copy the material |
| Theory | "Pedal de Re, roce frigio con Mib, ostinato 5 contra 4" | Do exactly that. If you see a problem, say it; don't "improve" it silently |

Don't ask for their level of theory. Read it from how they talk and answer in the same register: with someone who says "más oscuro", don't talk about "Phrygian modal mixture" unless they ask why. With someone who talks in chords, be precise.

## 1. Reflect before writing

For anything bigger than a small change, start with one sentence in their words, with the concrete decisions in brackets for whoever wants them:

> Lo leo como algo que respira mal y se va torciendo: grave y cerca, poco espacio, un pulso que cojea (Re menor con roce de Mib, 5 contra 4, respiraciones como percusión).

If the request is open, wait for a yes or a correction; a wrong reading costs a sentence instead of forty bars. If the request is precise, reflect and write in the same turn.

## 2. Ask by contrast, not in the abstract

Not "¿qué tempo quieres?" but "¿más como un latido lento o como una máquina que se acelera?". At most two questions, each with two concrete poles. Even better: two short contrasting sketches (4–8 bars) they can listen to, named in plain words ("más cerca", "más abierto").

## 3. Translate with levers

Emotion research shows that a few features carry most of the effect and that they add up: **mode** (major/minor) matters most for happy versus sad, then **tempo** and **register**; for fear versus calm, **register** and **dynamics** dominate and mode barely matters; articulation and timbre carry less of the basic emotion but most of the *style*. So for any sensation, decide these levers explicitly:

mode or harmonic colour · register · density (how many voices, how often) · tempo and pulse · dynamics and their shape · articulation · timbre · space (dry/near or wet/far) · harmonic rhythm · what stays unresolved.

`references/translation-table.md` has the common sensations and adjectives (in Spanish and English) with starting points for each lever, plus the relative adjustments ("más aire", "menos denso", "más sucio"). They are starting points, not recipes.

## 4. From a reference to techniques

When the user names a work, a series or a composer:

1. Say what defines it in techniques: instrumentation, rhythm, harmony, form, production. (For an experimental series opening: "contraste entre lo infantil y la amenaza, la voz como percusión, ritmos cojos, armonía mínima, cortes en seco".)
2. Say what you can reproduce with the sounds available and what will need the user's own sounds later.
3. Write something original with those techniques. Never reproduce its melodies, recognisable progressions or signature figures; take the language, not the text.

## 5. Watch the neighbouring cliché

Every intention has a neighbour that is easy to fall into and wrong. The ones that have actually happened:

- **"Unsettling", "uncanny"** → a 70s spy theme (Latin percussion, octave-jumping bass, horn-like hits) or a **creepy circus** (celesta, music box, harpsichord, fairground waltz). Childlike timbres read as "freak show" unless the brief is grotesque on purpose.
- **"Mysterious", "melancholic"** → **romantic sadness** (maj7 and add9 chords, the Neapolitan, a broken cadence, a cello with vibrato). Melancholy can be the undertone; it should rarely be the surface.
- **A flat 2nd over a major triad** (Eb and F natural over D major) → **Spanish or Arabic colour** (Phrygian dominant). Use it only if that is the point.
- **"Epic"** → generic trailer. **"Sad"** → a I–V–vi–IV ballad. **"Mysterious"** → a horror sting.

 Before handing back a first draft, ask yourself which neighbour you might have written and check it against the clichés listed for the style in the `styles` skill. When the user says "esto me recuerda a X", find the concrete elements that cause it and remove those, not everything.

## 6. Relative feedback

Users steer with relative words. Translate each into one or two small, concrete changes, make them, and say in one line what changed:

> "Más aire" → he quitado el colchón de órgano del 9 al 16 y he subido los violines a armónicos en Re6.

Make the smallest change that answers the request. If one word could mean two things ("más oscuro": lower register, or minor colour, or fewer high frequencies), pick the most likely, do it, and mention the other option.

## 7. Keep the intention visible

Write the intention as an annotation over the bars ("Infección: la máquina va perdiendo golpes, cada vuelta más contagiada"). Keep a song concept with its limits at the top of the song (see `four-hands-basics`) and update it when the user changes their mind. Every choice they make (this path, more intimate, less dense) is their decision as composer; your annotations make that visible in the score.

## 8. Don't deliver a whole piece at once

Unless they ask for it, go section by section, each one an intervention they can listen to and undo. Their reactions are the composition.
